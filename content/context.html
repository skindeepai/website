How can an AI keep working on one long job when its history no longer fits in its working memory? The same Qwen3.8-27B model ran each test below; within a test, every approach answered the same questions.

The most reliable approach so far keeps a small, pinned state in view and moves old text, word for word, to an archive the model can search. Approaches that only drop or summarise old text forget the distant past. And no approach kept a rule that was stated once at the start, until rules had their own permanent place in that pinned state.

Recommendations

What we would do today when building an agent whose history outgrows its window:

  1. Give the rules and the goal a permanent, pinned place at the top of the context, where no clean-up step and no edit can remove them, and tell the model to read it before answering. With no such place, four methods answered 0 of 20 rule questions the way the rule required; with permanent rule lines, 5 of 5. Over a 1,000,000-token run a pinned rule still lapsed 6 times in 28, so a reminder when a question is asked is the next thing to test. Keeping a rule. Outside studies found that a pinned head was the only compaction that kept rules intact.
  2. Put shared instructions in the system message and keep them unchanged, so the prefix cache can reuse them: a repeated 30K prompt started answering in 0.8 seconds instead of 11.4. How prefix caching works.
  3. Never summarise a summary. Outside studies find each round drifts further from the original, and summarising was the slowest method we ran. Keep the originals. What summaries keep and lose.
  4. Drop tool output whose effect already lives in the environment, but keep the verbatim text searchable in an archive with a search tool. Dropping it outright lost every “who did it” detail. Archive and search versus compaction.
  5. Give a method that edits inside the window at least 32K tokens of room. At 16K the batch, the state, the instructions and the thinking did not fit, and accuracy collapsed.
  6. Measure with questions planted at known distances back, and include a rule. End-of-run questions alone did not separate methods: at a 32K window three of four methods answered all 35 final questions while scoring from 16/30 to 28/30 on the planted ones. And the method with the best memory score kept the rule no better than the worst. The distance tables.

What we are trying to solve now

An agent that works on one task for weeks, with millions of tokens of history behind a capped live window, and stays fast and on goal. The first 1,000,000-token run held its accuracy, but it was stopped before its final check and showed three things to fix: an answers file that can be wiped, weak answers about what was true at an earlier point, and a pinned rule that lapses in use. Next come those fixes, a method that asks nothing of the agent, and the missing baselines. The problem, the model’s limits and the plan.
